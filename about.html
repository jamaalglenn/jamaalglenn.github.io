Executive

Board Director, CFO, and Private Markets Investor

Jamaal Glenn is a board director, CFO, and private markets investor whose work spans corporate governance, strategic finance, and private markets investing in the AI economy. He is Chief Financial Officer of SoLo Funds and founder and CEO of JG Holdings, an investment and strategic finance advisory firm.

Jamaal helps lead SoLo Funds, a financial technology company serving more than two million users and an Inc. 5000 honoree as one of America's fastest-growing private companies. As CFO, he has played a central role in doubling revenue and transforming the company into an AI-first organization. Through JG Holdings, he advises executive teams on governance and strategic finance, including as a fractional CFO, and advises institutional investors on private markets, drawing on his background as a venture capital investor.

Earlier in his career, Jamaal founded and exited a digital media company focused on politics and policy. He holds an MBA from Stanford Graduate School of Business, an MPA from Harvard Kennedy School, and bachelor's degrees in finance and journalism from the University of Missouri. He lives in the Washington, D.C., area with his wife and their young twins.

Board Director

An AI-Native Director With Deep Audit and Deal Experience

Jamaal brings the perspective of a sitting CFO, an investor, and a former audit committee chair to the boardroom. He has raised, invested, and negotiated more than $200 million in private market transactions across venture capital, growth equity, private credit, and acquisitions. As a finance leader, he has overseen financial reporting, internal controls, audit, enterprise risk, and capital allocation, and he qualifies as an audit committee financial expert. Having led a company's transformation into an AI-first organization, he brings AI-native judgment to technology oversight, one of the fastest-growing gaps on corporate boards.

Jamaal chairs the board of The Pivot Fund, which invests in local news and civic infrastructure. He serves on the board and finance committee of LION Publishers, where he previously chaired the audit committee and served as treasurer. He is a former director of the Center for Public Integrity.

He is a member of the Private Directors Association, where he serves in committee leadership, and a graduate of the Black Corporate Board Readiness program.

Trisector leader

Citizen Diplomacy, from Berlin to Bratislava

Jamaal Glenn is a trisector leader who works across business, government, and civil society. He believes many of the most consequential problems fall between sectors, where no single institution owns the solution and no market exists to fix them. Jamaal has built his career around that gap, pairing executive roles with sustained work alongside leaders in policy, diplomacy, and academia.

He is a member of the Council on Foreign Relations, a Marshall Memorial Fellow of the German Marshall Fund of the United States, and an alumnus of the Obama Foundation Leaders USA program. Jamaal was also selected for three further transatlantic programs: the U.S.-Spain Young Leaders Program, which hosts ten Americans each year; the New Urban Progress Fellowship, supported by the Alfred Herrhausen Gesellschaft; and the inaugural Transatlantic Digital Debates Fellowship with New America and the Global Public Policy Institute, where he was one of nine Americans chosen. Closer to home, he is a fellow of USMEXICO360, the U.S.-Mexico Foundation's citizen diplomacy program for leaders across business, government, media, and civil society in both countries.

Jamaal previously taught at New York University and the City University of New York, where he was a top-rated professor, teaching finance, marketing, and entrepreneurship to graduate students and executives at NYU's School of Professional Studies, and to undergraduates in Queens College's honors minor in Business and Liberal Arts.

These networks give Jamaal a global perspective and lasting relationships with leaders across government, business, and academia. He brings that cross-sector judgment to his work in corporate governance, strategic finance, and AI.

Thought leader and Speaker

Work, AI, and Money, Before It's Conventional Wisdom

Jamaal writes about the future of work, AI, and money: how AI changes who gets hired and paid, how much leverage workers really have, what Wall Street is signaling, and what education should prepare people for. He writes as an operator who manages AI spending and AI agents inside a business, which gives him little patience for hype.

In Jamaal's Letter, his newsletter, he offers strong opinions, frameworks, and predictions. His widely cited take on AI token budgets argues that they are a business cost, not employee compensation, and his writing on "corporate AI theater" challenges how companies use AI announcements as marketing.

His analysis and commentary have appeared in or been featured by TIME, Fortune, TechCrunch, Adweek, and L'Express, and he brings the same unsentimental view to boardrooms, conferences, universities, and podcasts.

My Story

A Corner Booth at McDonald's

I remember the day my father graduated from law school. I was six years old, too small to see the stage past the people in front of me, so I watched him on a projector screen from the back of the overflow section. When they announced his name, my mother cheered so loudly that I was embarrassed. His cheering section was just us: my mother, my baby brother, and me.

My father had returned to law school in his mid-forties, balancing night classes, a full-time job, and a young family. My mother taught sixth-grade social studies. Between them, education was non-negotiable. They paid me to read books, summer vacation became an extension of the classroom, and at dinner, every opinion had to be defended.

After law school, my father joined the Denver City Attorney's office. Before dropping me at school, we sat in the same corner booth at McDonald's. He drank coffee, I ate hash browns, and we talked about how he used the law to serve the city. He even helped me plan my first run for student council. I didn't realize it then, but those conversations were about service, responsibility, and legacy.

My father died of non-Hodgkin's lymphoma five days after my tenth birthday. The ten years I had with him still shape me.

I discovered writing in seventh grade, recounting Denver Nuggets games in a daily journal, and later edited my high school newspaper. At the University of Missouri, I studied journalism and finance. Wall Street didn't recruit there, but Sponsors for Educational Opportunity got me in the door as an analyst at J.P. Morgan.

I spent three years there, through the height of the financial crisis. Colleagues who had spent decades on Wall Street would tell me they'd never seen markets behave like this. Watching them convulse, I formed strong convictions about where Washington and Wall Street collide, and I started writing about them. I taught myself web development, built a site, and found an audience. By 2009, it was a business.

I was running that company when Stanford called. I phoned my mother, who was walking near the monuments in Washington. She screamed, "Jamaal got into Stanford!" Strangers gave her a thumbs-up.

Stanford, and later Harvard Kennedy School, widened my view of the world. They were also a study in contrasts. The names on Harvard's buildings belong to people who died decades ago; at Stanford, many belong to people still alive. My business school classmates had extraordinary analytical horsepower and drive. My Kennedy School classmates had something rarer: genuine idealism about improving the world. I've spent my career since trying to hold both.

Years later, when I told my mother I'd be teaching at NYU, she said, "Finally, you have a job that I understand." She spent her career in a public school classroom. I've spent mine in finance, investing, and boardrooms, and the part she recognized was the teaching.

My wife and I spent six years trying to become parents before our son and daughter arrived together. We still marvel that they're ours. I think often about how much to cushion them from the world and how much to prepare them for it through challenge, the way my parents did at our dinner table.
