Executive

Building at the intersection of finance and technology

Jamaal Glenn is an executive, board director, investor, and educator whose work sits at the intersection of corporate governance, strategic finance, and AI. He is Chief Financial Officer of SoLo Funds and founder and CEO of JG Holdings, an investment and advisory firm.

Jamaal helps lead SoLo Funds, a community finance platform serving more than two million users and an Inc. 5000 honoree as one of America's fastest-growing private companies. As CFO, he has played a central role in doubling revenue, reaching profitability, and rebuilding the company as an AI-first organization. Through JG Holdings, he advises executive teams on corporate governance and strategic finance, and institutional investors on investment strategy.

Earlier in his career, Jamaal founded and exited a digital media company focused on politics and policy.

He chairs the board of The Pivot Fund, which invests in local news and civic infrastructure, and serves on the board of LION Publishers, where he previously chaired the audit committee and served as treasurer. He writes Jamaal's Letter, a newsletter on AI, finance, work, and power, and has taught at New York University and the City University of New York.

Jamaal holds an MBA from Stanford Graduate School of Business, an MPA from Harvard Kennedy School, and bachelor's degrees in finance and journalism from the University of Missouri.

Jamaal Glenn is a trisector leader who works across business, government, and civil society. He believes many of the most consequential problems fall between sectors, where no single institution owns the solution and no market exists to fix them. Jamaal has built his career around that gap, pairing executive roles with sustained work alongside leaders in policy, diplomacy, and academia.

He is a member of the Council on Foreign Relations, a Marshall Memorial Fellow of the German Marshall Fund of the United States, and an alumnus of the Obama Foundation Leaders USA program. Jamaal was also selected for three further transatlantic programs: the U.S.-Spain Young Leaders Program, which hosts ten Americans each year; the New Urban Progress Fellowship, supported by the Alfred Herrhausen Gesellschaft; and the inaugural Transatlantic Digital Debates Fellowship with New America and the Global Public Policy Institute, where he was one of nine Americans chosen.

These networks give Jamaal a global perspective and lasting relationships with leaders across government, business, and academia. He brings that cross-sector judgment to his work in corporate governance, strategic finance, and AI.

Thought leader and speaker

Ideas tested against operating reality

Jamaal Glenn writes about the economics of AI: what it costs, what it does to work, who captures the value, and how companies and boards should respond. He writes from the operator's seat, managing inference budgets and portfolios of AI agents across finance, operations, and go-to-market.

In Jamaal's Letter, his newsletter, Jamaal offers strong opinions, frameworks, and predictions on AI, finance, work, and power. His widely cited take on AI token budgets argues that they are a business cost, not employee compensation, and his writing on "corporate AI theater" challenges how companies use AI announcements as marketing.

His analysis and commentary have appeared in or been featured by TIME, Fortune, TechCrunch, Adweek, and L'Express, and he brings the same hype-free view of AI to boardrooms, conferences, universities, and podcasts.

About Jamaal

My Story

I remember the day my father graduated from law school. I was six years old, too small to see the stage past the people in front of me, so I watched him on a projector screen from the back of the overflow section. When they announced his name, my mother cheered so loudly that I was embarrassed. His cheering section was just us: my mother, my baby brother, and me.

My father had returned to law school in his mid-forties, balancing night classes, a full-time job, and a young family. My mother taught sixth-grade social studies. Between them, education was non-negotiable. They paid me to read books, summer vacation became an extension of the classroom, and at dinner, every opinion had to be defended.

After law school, my father joined the Denver City Attorney's office. Before dropping me at school, we sat in the same corner booth at McDonald's. He drank coffee, I ate hash browns, and we talked about how he used the law to serve the city. He even helped me plan my first run for student council. I didn't realize it then, but those conversations were about service, responsibility, and legacy.

My father died of non-Hodgkin's lymphoma five days after my tenth birthday. The ten years I had with him still shape me.

I discovered writing in seventh grade, recounting Denver Nuggets games in a daily journal, and later edited my high school newspaper. At the University of Missouri, I studied journalism and finance. Wall Street didn't recruit there, but Sponsors for Educational Opportunity got me in the door as an analyst at J.P. Morgan.

I spent three years there, through the height of the financial crisis. Trading-floor veterans would tell me they'd never, in twenty years, seen the yield curve look like this. Watching markets convulse, I formed strong convictions about where Washington and Wall Street collide, and I started writing about them. I taught myself web development, built a site, and found an audience. By 2009, it was a business.

I was running that company when Stanford called. I phoned my mother, who was walking near the monuments in Washington. She screamed, "Jamaal got into Stanford!" Strangers gave her a thumbs-up.

Stanford, and later Harvard Kennedy School, widened my view of the world. They were also a study in contrasts. The names on Harvard's buildings belong to people who died decades ago; at Stanford, many belong to people still alive. My business school classmates had extraordinary analytical horsepower and drive. My Kennedy School classmates had something rarer: genuine idealism about improving the world. I've spent my career since trying to hold both.

After six years of trying to become parents, my wife and I welcomed twins. We still marvel that they're ours. I think often about how much to cushion them from the world and how much to prepare them for it through challenge, the way my parents did at our dinner table.
